Overview

In this homework we built a physically based renderer that uses Monte Carlo path tracing. We started with the core pipeline: generating camera rays through randomly sampled points in each pixel and intersecting them with triangles and spheres. We then added a bounding volume hierarchy (BVH), which cuts the number of intersection tests per ray from thousands to a handful, so meshes with hundreds of thousands of triangles render in a fraction of a second. On top of that we implemented direct lighting in two ways (uniform hemisphere sampling and light importance sampling), and then full global illumination with recursive bounces and Russian Roulette path termination. Finally, we added adaptive sampling, which stops sampling a pixel once its estimate has statistically converged.

The main thing we took away is how each image is just an average of many random estimates of the rendering equation. Nearly every design choice (importance sampling lights, cosine-weighted bounce directions, Russian Roulette, adaptive sampling) is a way to get the same expected value with less variance or less work. Small bookkeeping details mattered a lot: dividing by the right PDF, keeping track of world vs. local coordinates, and offsetting new rays by EPS_F so they do not hit the surface they start on.

Problems we ran into: when every primitive's centroid fell on the same side of the split, the BVH recursed forever, so we added a median-split fallback (Part 2). We also found that the starter camera frames the Cornell box scenes for a 480×360 window; at other resolutions the light leaves the frame. So all of our renders use -r 480 360 (the dragon uses 480×480, like the spec).

Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray generation

PathTracer::raytrace_pixel(x, y) takes ns_aa samples for pixel \((x, y)\). For each sample it adds a random offset in \([0,1)^2\) from gridSampler to the pixel's bottom-left corner. It then divides by the image width and height to get normalized coordinates \((u, v) \in [0,1]^2\), and asks the camera for a ray through that point. The ray's depth is set to max_ray_depth, it is traced with est_radiance_global_illumination, and the average radiance over all samples is written to sampleBuffer.

Camera::generate_ray(u, v) maps \((u, v)\) onto a virtual sensor one unit in front of the pinhole, on the plane \(z = -1\) in camera space. The bottom-left corner of the sensor is \((-\tan(\frac{hFov}{2}), -\tan(\frac{vFov}{2}), -1)\) and the top-right corner is \((\tan(\frac{hFov}{2}), \tan(\frac{vFov}{2}), -1)\), so

\[ d_{cam} = \big((2u-1)\tan(\frac{hFov}{2}),\ (2v-1)\tan(\frac{vFov}{2}),\ -1\big). \]

The field-of-view angles are stored in degrees, so they are converted to radians first. The direction is rotated into world space with the camera-to-world matrix c2w and normalized. The ray starts at the camera position, and its valid interval is set to [nClip, fClip].

Primitive intersection

Each ray carries a valid interval [min_t, max_t]. When a primitive finds a hit at time \(t\) inside that interval, it sets r.max_t = t. Any later primitive must then be closer to count, so after testing everything the ray holds the nearest hit. intersect also fills the Intersection record: t, the surface normal n, the primitive hit, and its bsdf.

Triangles (Möller–Trumbore). A point on the triangle can be written in barycentric coordinates as \((1-b_1-b_2)p_1 + b_1 p_2 + b_2 p_3\). Setting this equal to the ray \(o + t d\) gives a 3×3 linear system in \((t, b_1, b_2)\). With \(e_1 = p_2 - p_1\), \(e_2 = p_3 - p_1\), \(s = o - p_1\), \(s_1 = d \times e_2\), \(s_2 = s \times e_1\), Cramer's rule gives

\[ \begin{bmatrix} t \\ b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{s_1 \cdot e_1} \begin{bmatrix} s_2 \cdot e_2 \\ s_1 \cdot s \\ s_2 \cdot d \end{bmatrix}. \]

If \(s_1 \cdot e_1 \approx 0\), the ray is parallel to the triangle and there is no hit. Otherwise the hit is valid when \(b_1 \ge 0\), \(b_2 \ge 0\), \(b_1 + b_2 \le 1\) (the point is inside the triangle) and \(t \in [\text{min_t}, \text{max_t}]\). The shading normal interpolates the three vertex normals with the same barycentric weights: \(n = (1-b_1-b_2)n_1 + b_1 n_2 + b_2 n_3\), then normalized.

Spheres. Substituting the ray into \(|p - c|^2 = r^2\) gives the quadratic \(at^2 + bt + c' = 0\) with \(a = d\cdot d\), \(b = 2(o-c)\cdot d\), \(c' = |o-c|^2 - r^2\). A negative discriminant means a miss. Otherwise we take the smaller root if it is inside the valid interval, else the larger root (the ray starts inside the sphere). The normal is \((p - c)/|p - c|\).

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

Construction

BVHAccel::construct_bvh works recursively on a range of primitives [start, end):

  1. Compute the bounding box of all primitives in the range, plus a second box that holds only their centroids. Create a node with the first box.
  2. If the range has at most max_leaf_size primitives, the node is a leaf: store start and end and return.
  3. Otherwise pick the split axis as the axis along which the centroid box is longest, i.e. where the primitives are most spread out.
  4. Split point heuristic: the average of the primitives' centroids along that axis. std::partition reorders the range in place so primitives with centroid below the average come first. This needs no extra memory and gives the children iterator ranges directly.
  5. If the partition leaves one side empty (e.g. many primitives share the same centroid), recursing would loop forever on the same range. In that case we fall back to a median split: std::nth_element puts the middle primitive (by centroid) at the midpoint, so both halves are nonempty.
  6. Recurse on [start, mid) and [mid, end) for the left and right children.

We chose the mean centroid over the spatial midpoint because it adapts to where the geometry actually is. For a mesh with a dense region (like a face) and sparse regions, it splits through the dense part instead of leaving most triangles on one side.

Intersection

BBox::intersect uses the slab method. For each axis it computes the two times the ray crosses the box's planes, \(t = (\text{min}_i - o_i)/d_i\) and \((\text{max}_i - o_i)/d_i\), and swaps them if \(d_i < 0\). The ray is inside the box during the overlap of the three axis intervals and the ray's own \([t_0, t_1]\). If the overlap is empty it is a miss; otherwise \(t_0, t_1\) are updated. A ray that starts inside the box still counts as a hit.

The BVH traversal first tests the node's box (using copies of min_t/max_t so the ray is not modified) and skips the whole subtree on a miss. In a leaf it tests every primitive. has_intersection returns as soon as anything is hit, since shadow rays only need a yes/no. intersect must visit both children, because the first child's hit is not necessarily the closest. The primitives update max_t, so later tests in the other child only accept closer hits.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Both direct lighting functions estimate the reflection equation at a hit point \(p\) with outgoing direction \(\omega_o\):

\[ L_{out}(p, \omega_o) = \int_{H^2} f(\omega_i \to \omega_o)\, L_{in}(p, \omega_i) \cos\theta_i \, d\omega_i \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \frac{f(\omega_j \to \omega_o) L_{in}(p, \omega_j) \cos\theta_j}{p(\omega_j)}. \]

The diffuse BSDF is \(f = \rho / \pi\), where \(\rho\) is the reflectance. Both functions work in a local frame where the surface normal is \(+z\), using o2w/w2o, so \(\cos\theta_i\) is just the \(z\) component of the local \(\omega_i\). Zero-bounce radiance returns the emission of whatever the camera ray hit, which makes the light itself visible.

Uniform hemisphere sampling. We draw num_samples directions uniformly from the hemisphere around the normal, so \(p(\omega) = 1/2\pi\). For each direction we convert it to world space and cast a ray from the hit point (with min_t = EPS_F). If the ray hits something, we use that surface's emission as \(L_{in}\) (it is zero unless we hit a light). We add \(f \cdot L_{in} \cos\theta / (1/2\pi)\) and divide the sum by the number of samples.

Light importance sampling. We loop over every light in the scene. A point light is sampled once (every sample would be identical); an area light is sampled ns_area_light times. sample_L returns a direction toward a point on the light, the distance to it, its radiance and the PDF of choosing that direction. We skip the sample if the light is behind the surface (\(\cos\theta \le 0\)). Otherwise we cast a shadow ray with min_t = EPS_F and max_t = distToLight - EPS_F. If nothing blocks it, we add \(f \cdot L \cos\theta / p\). Each light's sum is divided by its sample count, and the lights are added together.

Soft shadows on CBbunny.dae with light importance sampling, 1 sample per pixel:

With 1 light ray, each pixel either sees the light or does not, so the soft shadow under the bunny is a speckle of black and lit pixels. As the number of light rays goes up, each pixel averages over more points on the area light. The penumbra becomes a smooth gradient from full shadow next to the bunny to fully lit further away, and the noise on the floor and walls drops roughly like \(1/\sqrt{N}\).

Hemisphere vs. light sampling. With the same number of samples, uniform hemisphere sampling is much noisier. The area light covers only a small part of the hemisphere, so most sampled directions hit a wall and add nothing, while the few that hit the light add a large value. That high variance shows up as grain everywhere, especially in shadows. Light sampling only picks directions that point at the light, so almost every sample contributes and only visibility varies. This gives clean soft shadows at the same sample count. Light sampling can also render point lights, which hemisphere sampling never hits. Both estimators converge to the same answer. In our renders the hemisphere images are grainy across every wall at 64 samples per pixel, while the importance-sampled images are already smooth, with clean shadows under the bunny and spheres.

Part 4: Global Illumination

Sampling the BSDF. DiffuseBSDF::sample_f draws \(\omega_i\) from a cosine-weighted hemisphere, with \(p(\omega_i) = \cos\theta_i / \pi\), writes the direction and PDF through the pointers, and returns \(f = \rho/\pi\). Cosine weighting sends more samples toward the normal, where the \(\cos\theta\) factor makes contributions largest.

Indirect lighting. est_radiance_global_illumination intersects the camera ray with the scene. At the hit point it adds zero-bounce radiance, then calls at_least_one_bounce_radiance. The ray's depth field counts how many bounces are left; camera rays start at max_ray_depth. With -m 0 only the zero-bounce term is returned. at_least_one_bounce_radiance(r, isect) does the following:

  1. Compute direct lighting at the hit with one_bounce_radiance. With isAccumBounces = true this is always added. With isAccumBounces = false it is only added at the last bounce (depth == 1), so the image shows only light that bounced exactly max_ray_depth times.
  2. If depth == 1, stop.
  3. Russian Roulette: continue with probability 0.65 (termination probability 0.35). The first indirect bounce from a camera ray is always traced, as the spec requires.
  4. Sample \(\omega_i\) with sample_f, convert it to world space, and spawn a ray from the hit point with min_t = EPS_F and depth - 1.
  5. If that ray hits the scene, recurse, and add \(f \cdot L_{rec} \cos\theta_i / (p(\omega_i) \cdot p_{continue})\). Dividing by the continuation probability keeps the estimator unbiased: paths that survive are weighted up to make up for the paths that were cut.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

Different pixels need very different numbers of samples. A pixel that sees the light, or a plain directly lit wall, converges after a few samples, while pixels in soft shadows or lit mostly by indirect light stay noisy for much longer. Adaptive sampling tracks each pixel's samples and stops as soon as it is confident the pixel's mean has converged, which spends the sample budget where it is needed.

In raytrace_pixel, for every sample \(k\) we compute its illuminance \(x_k\) with Vector3D::illum() and keep two running sums, \(s_1 = \sum x_k\) and \(s_2 = \sum x_k^2\). Every samplesPerBatch samples we compute

\[ \mu = \frac{s_1}{n}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\left(s_2 - \frac{s_1^2}{n}\right), \qquad I = 1.96 \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}. \]

\(I\) is the half-width of the 95% confidence interval for the pixel's true mean. If \(I \le \text{maxTolerance} \cdot \mu\), we stop early. Otherwise we keep sampling until ns_aa samples. The pixel's color is the average of the samples actually taken, and that count is written to sampleCountBuffer, which produces the sampling rate image (red = many samples, blue = few). Checking only once per batch keeps the extra cost small.

2048 max samples per pixel, batch size 64, tolerance 0.05, 1 light ray, max ray depth 5:
